# 🔗 Systems Thinking in Training

> **Training problems rarely exist in isolation. I look at the relationships between people, processes, information, technology, and learning to understand what is really happening before deciding what to change.**

---

## Why I Use a Systems Perspective

A training request often arrives as a simple statement:

> "We need training."

But that statement does not always tell us what the real need is.

The underlying issue might involve:

- a new process
- unfamiliar technology
- unclear instructions
- inconsistent information
- insufficient practice
- a difficult workflow
- limited access to support
- a gap between training and the actual work

If I treat the request as only a course-development task, I may solve the wrong problem.

My first step is therefore to understand the **system around the learning**.

---

## 🔗 The Elements I Examine

I look at five connected areas.

### 👥 People

I consider the people involved in the work and in the learning process.

This includes:

- learners
- instructors or facilitators
- subject-matter experts
- managers or supervisors
- support staff
- people responsible for maintaining information or systems

I want to understand what each person needs to know, do, provide, or support.

---

### ⚙️ Processes

I look at how the work actually happens.

Questions I ask include:

- What happens first?
- What happens next?
- Where are decisions made?
- Where are handoffs between people or systems?
- Where might a learner become stuck?
- Which steps depend on another step being completed correctly?

A process can create a learning need even when the learning content itself is accurate.

---

### 📚 Information

Learning depends on information being available, understandable, and current.

I look at:

- instructions
- procedures
- reference material
- learner records
- course information
- documentation
- feedback
- support questions
- performance information

When information changes, I consider what other learning or support materials may also need to change.

---

### 💻 Technology

Technology can enable learning, but it can also introduce friction.

I consider:

- which systems people use
- how the systems fit into the workflow
- what the learner needs to know about them
- where users may need additional guidance
- whether a technology change affects existing learning materials

My experience with **MoodleCloud, Camtasia, Zoho, and digital learning environments** has reinforced this perspective.

I don't assume that knowing a tool is the same as solving a training problem.

The more important question is:

> **How does the technology support the work and the learning?**

---

### 🧠 Learning

Finally, I consider what the person actually needs to learn.

That may include:

- understanding a concept
- following a process
- using software
- making a decision
- practicing a skill
- applying knowledge in a workplace situation
- finding information at the point of need

The learning intervention should connect back to the task.

---

## 🔄 Relationships Matter

The five areas above are not separate boxes.

They interact.

A change in one part of the system can create consequences elsewhere.

For example:

```

Technology Change
       │
       ▼
Process Changes
       │
       ▼
Instructions Need Updating
       │
       ▼
Learning Content May Need Updating
       │
       ▼
Learners Encounter Questions
       │
       ▼
Support Requests Increase
       │
       ▼
New Evidence for Improvement

```

**This is why I look beyond the original training request.**

> The visible problem may be at one point in the system while the underlying cause sits somewhere else.


---
## 🔍 From Symptoms to Causes

One of the most useful questions I ask is:

> **Is this the problem, or is this evidence of another problem?**

For example, imagine that employees repeatedly ask how to complete the same task.

The immediate response may be to answer the question.

That is necessary.

But I would also want to understand the pattern.

Is the task difficult?

Is the instruction unclear?

Is the process new?

Has the software changed?

Is the relevant information difficult to find?

Was the task sufficiently practiced?

Does the training reflect the current workflow?

The repeated question becomes a signal that helps me investigate the system.


---
##  🧭 Finding the Point of Friction

I look for places where the learner or employee has to stop, search, guess, repeat, or ask for help.

These points of friction can occur:

**Before learning**

- unclear expectations
- difficult scheduling
- missing information
- confusing communication

**During learning**

- unclear explanations
- excessive information
- insufficient practice
- technology difficulties

**After learning**

- difficulty applying the skill
- missing job aids
- outdated documentation
- recurring support questions
- changes in the workplace process

The purpose of identifying friction is not to assign blame.

It is to understand where the system is hindering performance or making successful performance harder.


---
## 🎯 Choosing Where to Intervene

Once I understand the relationships, I can consider where an intervention is most useful.

The response might involve:
| Observation               | Possible response                        |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Knowledge gap             | Learning activity                        |
| Difficult task            | Guided practice                          |
| Complex procedure         | Job aid or reference guide               |
| Software difficulty       | Demonstration or targeted support        |
| Unclear information       | Rewrite or reorganize documentation      |
| Process problem           | Process clarification or adjustment      |
| Recurring support issue   | Improve learning or self-service support |
| Multiple connected issues | Combination of interventions             |

This is important to my approach:

> **I do not assume that every problem requires more training.**

The goal is to address the point in the system that is creating the difficulty.


---
## 🧩 A Practical Analysis Sequence
When I examine a training situation, I can move through a simple sequence:

```
1. UNDERSTAND
   What is happening?

        ↓

2. CONNECT
   Which people, processes, information,
   technologies, and learning elements are involved?

        ↓

3. IDENTIFY
   Where is the friction?

        ↓

4. INVESTIGATE
   What may be causing it?

        ↓

5. DECIDE
   Where is the most useful intervention?

        ↓

6. OBSERVE
   What happens after the change?

        ↓

7. IMPROVE
   What should be adjusted next?
```
> [!TIP]
> This gives me a practical way to move from a training request toward an informed response.


---
## 📊 Evidence Helps Complete the Picture

I also look for evidence rather than relying only on assumptions.

Depending on the situation, useful evidence may include:

- learner feedback
- completion information
- LMS activity
- recurring support questions
- assessment results
- observations from facilitators
- learner or employee performance
- content errors
- documentation changes
- recurring points of confusion

My background in Economics, data analysis, learning analytics, and LMS analytics supports this part of my approach.

The purpose of measurement is not to collect data for its own sake.

It is to help answer:

> **What does the evidence tell me about what is working, what is not working, and where attention is needed?**


---
## 🌱 Systems Thinking Is About Better Decisions

For me, systems thinking is not about creating complicated diagrams or adding another layer of process.

It is a way of asking better questions.

Instead of:

> "What training should we create?"

I ask:

> "What needs to change in the work, and what role should learning play in that change?"

Instead of:

> "Why are people asking so many questions?"

I ask:

> "What are those questions telling us about the system?"

Instead of:

> "Which technology should we use?"

I ask:

> "Where can technology make the learning or the work easier?"

That shift—from isolated tasks to relationships—helps me make more deliberate training decisions.


---
## 🔗 The Core Idea

My systems-thinking approach can be summarized in one sentence:

> Understand the connections before deciding on the solution.

People, processes, information, technology, and learning influence one another.

> [!TIP]
> When I understand those relationships, I can design training and support that are more closely connected to the work people actually need to do.
